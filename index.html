    Neighbourhoods are Statistics Canada dissemination areas (DAs), each home to roughly 400 to 700 people. Each is named after the two streets with the most length inside it. Values marked n/a were suppressed by Statistics Canada to protect privacy.

    Incomes are for the 2020 calendar year, when pandemic benefits raised some low incomes. City figures are Statistics Canada's own figures for the City of Guelph. Most measures past population and age come from the 25% long-form sample, so small neighbourhoods can swing a lot. Work figures describe May 2021, during the pandemic.

    Data: Statistics Canada, 2021 Census of Population (Statistics Canada Open Licence). Crime: Guelph Police Service calls for service, 2021–2025. Map, addresses and city boundary: City of Guelph Open Data. The 15-minute score comes from 15-Minute Guelph. An independent project, not an official City of Guelph service. Created by Randy Harris.

    Crime figures come from Guelph Police Service calls for service, 2021 to 2025 (about 400,000 calls). The app keeps 51,909 calls about place-based incidents in five groups: violence and threats; break-ins and vehicle theft; theft and vandalism; disturbances and disorder; and collisions and driving complaints. It leaves out administrative records, 911 hang-ups, traffic stops and other police-initiated work, wellness and mental-health calls, domestic calls, missing persons, fraud and sex offences, because they are not tied to a place or would be unfair to map. Calls give a street but no house number, so each street's calls are spread along it (half by length, half by the number of addresses on each block). Each home gets the calls a year on streets within 400 m, a 5-minute walk; a neighbourhood's figure is the average of its homes. Vehicle-theft calls logged at Guelph Police headquarters (15 Wyndham St S) are dropped. Calls are not convictions, and long arterial roads blur where along them a call happened.

    Why it matters for wellbeing: crime and the fear of it affect health beyond the harm to victims, discouraging walking and outdoor activity and weakening ties between neighbours (Lorenc et al., 2013; Statistics Canada, perceptions of personal safety). Links between crime and census measures in the Relate chart describe places, not people, and do not show that one causes the other.
